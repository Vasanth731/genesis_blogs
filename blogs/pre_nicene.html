Chapter 4 : The Pre-Nicene Era

The history of the early church is, in many ways, a story of continuity. From the apostles onward, leadership passed from one generation of bishops to the next, forming an unbroken line that traces back to the disciples and to Jesus himself. This apostolic succession is more than a historical curiosity — it reflects a belief that the Holy Spirit has been guiding the church consistently since its founding.

That continuity also shapes how we think about Scripture. The Bible we read today didn't fall from the sky fully formed. It took shape gradually, and it was the church — through councils, tradition, and liturgical practice — that determined which books belonged in the canon and how they should be understood. In other words, Scripture and tradition were never meant to be separated. The Protestant idea of "Scripture alone" overlooks the fact that the very process of assembling the Bible depended on the church's tradition.

This matters practically. Many early Christian writings and letters have survived alongside the Biblical texts, offering context from people who lived close to the apostles — sometimes people who had personally known them, or even known Jesus. Their faith wasn't an abstract, textual exercise; it was lived experience. That's part of why tradition carries weight: it isn't a rival to Scripture, but a companion to it.

The years following the apostles' deaths were turbulent. There was no finalized Bible yet — just scattered documents and oral teaching, held together by trust in what had been passed down from the apostles. The early church clung to four things, as described in Acts 2: prayer, fellowship, the apostles' teaching, and the breaking of bread. When persecution intensified (dramatically after the fall of Jerusalem in 70 AD), many early leaders were martyred — reportedly 22 of the first 28 popes. Faith, in that era, often demanded everything.

It's worth pausing on that. The people closest to Jesus in time lived with an intensity of conviction that can feel distant from ordinary faith today. That gap is worth sitting with, even without a tidy answer.

A common objection to church tradition asks: didn't Jesus only say what's written in the Bible? But the Gospel of John itself admits it doesn't record everything Jesus did or said. Paul explicitly tells the Thessalonians to hold on to the traditions they were taught — not only what was written but what was spoken. He also calls the church itself "the pillar and foundation of truth" — not the Bible in isolation, but the church as the living body sustaining it.

On the question of science and faith, the tension is often overstated. Augustine, writing centuries before Darwin, suggested that creation had the potential to unfold over time — a striking anticipation of evolutionary thinking, long before it was a scientific theory. The church's traditional stance isn't that the Bible must always be read literally; parts of Genesis, for instance, are written in the style of Hebrew poetry. Where a scientific claim is well-established, Scripture should be read accordingly — since two genuine truths cannot ultimately contradict each other.

Much of the perceived science-versus-religion conflict actually traces back to the French Revolution, when Enlightenment philosophers sought to elevate "Reason" as a replacement for religious authority, going so far as to stage a formal "cult of Reason." That was a philosophical and political rupture, not a scientific discovery that disproved faith.

As for the historical existence of Jesus, the manuscript evidence dwarfs that of almost any other ancient figure — reportedly enough to stack roughly 800 meters high, far surpassing the documentary record for someone like Alexander the Great. Skepticism about Jesus's historical existence isn't really rooted in a lack of evidence; it's often driven by other assumptions.

For anyone wanting a clear, accessible starting point on why the Christian faith holds together intellectually, Mere Christianity by C.S. Lewis remains a reliable recommendation — written by an Oxford scholar who once approached faith with skepticism himself.
