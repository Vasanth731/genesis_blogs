Chapter 6 : Clarifying the Mystery — Reflections on Ecumenical Councils Eight Through Fourteen

One of the more debated additions to Christian doctrine is the "filioque" — the belief that the Holy Spirit proceeds from both the Father and the Son, rather than from the Father alone, as the earliest form of the Apostles' Creed states. This clarification emerged largely in response to Arianism, a heresy that diminished the divinity of Christ. Even a careful reading of John's Gospel — "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God" — leaves room for ambiguity if read outside the church's broader tradition. Arius took that ambiguity and argued that Christ, though divine in some sense, was not eternal like the Father — a kind of subordinate, created intermediary rather than fully God.

The Catholic response was to insist that Father, Son, and Holy Spirit are three persons sharing one divine nature — co-eternal and consubstantial. The relationships between them (the Son "begotten of" the Father, the Spirit "proceeding from" the Father and the Son) aren't meant as literal timelines but as ways of describing eternal relationships within the one God. This understanding of the Trinity is itself a post-Easter revelation — it was only after the Resurrection that Christ commissioned the apostles to baptize "In the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit." That same commissioning also established a structure of teaching authority — what the Catholic Church calls the Magisterium — carried forward through apostolic succession, with bishops understood as the apostles' successors.

Arianism wasn't a fringe idea; at points, it held the support of a majority of bishops, and figures like Saint Athanasius spent much of their lives defending Christ's full divinity against it. The eventual doctrinal resolution that Christ is fully human and fully divine, united in one person without division or confusion is known as Chalcedonian Christology. It rules out any notion of a dividing line within Christ, some "more divine" or "more human" part. Instead, the incarnation is understood to begin at conception, not at the start of Jesus's public ministry which is why Mary is given the title Theotokos, "God-bearer." Settling one question, though, tended to open others: if Christ is one divine person with a human nature, did he have one will or two? Each answer clarified something further, which is simply how doctrine develops — not by inventing new beliefs, but by working out the full implications of what was always believed.

This same sense of continuity extends to the priesthood itself. Catholic tradition traces the priesthood not to the Levitical order but to the older, mysterious order of Melchizedek, the king and priest in Genesis who blesses Abraham. In Christ, that ancient thread and the Abrahamic one are drawn together, uniting Jew and Gentile once again. That same instinct for preserving history explains the church's regard for texts like the deuterocanonical books, preserved by Greek-speaking Jewish communities and covering the roughly 300-year gap between the Old and New Testaments. While not used as the primary basis for doctrine, they offer valuable theological continuity — writers like Irenaeus and Ignatius of Antioch drew on them to argue points of faith.

It's also worth remembering how the New Testament came to be written at all. The apostles weren't followed by reporters taking notes. The Gospels were written only after the Resurrection reshaped how everything that came before was understood. Many of the apostles, including Peter, were not formally literate; their accounts were preserved and written down by others, sometimes with real historical skill. Luke, a physician, is noted for the careful, evidence-minded detail in his Gospel. These were, by and large, ordinary people, who nonetheless carried the faith across enormous distances — from Thomas's traditional journey to Kerala, to Francis Xavier's missions from Europe to Japan centuries later.

Through all of this, one thread holds: Christian love is not sentiment but sacrifice — an act of the will rather than a feeling. It's a demanding tradition, but a remarkably enduring one — carried forward not by ease, but by centuries of ordinary people choosing extraordinary faithfulness.
