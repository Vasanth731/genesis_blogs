Pre Nicene Era: Lessons from the Early Church
The history of the early church is, in many ways, a story of continuity. From the apostles onward, leadership passed from one generation of bishops to the next, forming an unbroken line that traces back to the disciples and to Jesus himself. This apostolic succession is more than a historical curiosity — it reflects a belief that the Holy Spirit has been guiding the church consistently since its founding.
That continuity also shapes how we think about Scripture. The Bible we read today didn't fall from the sky fully formed. It took shape gradually, and it was the church through councils and  tradition that determined which books belonged in the canon and how they should be understood. In other words, Scripture and tradition were never meant to be separated.
Many early Christian writings and letters have survived alongside the Biblical texts, offering context from people who lived close to the apostles — sometimes people who had personally known them, or even known Jesus. Their faith wasn't an abstract, textual exercise; it was lived experience. That's part of why tradition carries weight: it isn't a rival to Scripture, but a companion to it.
The years following the apostles' deaths were turbulent. There was no finalized Bible yet — just scattered documents and oral teaching, held together by trust in what had been passed down from the apostles. The early church clung to four things, as described in Acts 2: prayer, fellowship, the apostles' teaching, and the breaking of bread. When persecution intensified (dramatically after the fall of Jerusalem in 70 AD), many early leaders were martyred (reportedly 22 of the first 28 popes). It's worth pausing on the thought that Faith, in that era, often demanded everything. The people closest to Jesus in time lived with an intensity of conviction that can feel distant from ordinary faith today. That gap is worth sitting with, even without a tidy answer.
A common objection to church tradition asks: didn't Jesus only say what's written in the Bible? But the Gospel of John itself admits it doesn't record everything Jesus did or said. Paul explicitly tells “So then, brothers and sisters, stand firm and hold fast to the teachings we passed on to you, whether by word of mouth or by letter” [2 Thessalonians 2:15]. He also calls the church itself "the pillar and foundation of truth" not the Bible in isolation, but the church as the living body sustaining it.
